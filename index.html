Simultaneous Learning of Static and Dynamic Charges

Philipp Stärk, H. Stooß, M. F. Langer, E. Rumiantsev, A. Schlaich, M. Ceriotti, P. Loche

Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics 28 (26), 16249–16258, 2026

DOI → Scholar

Long-range interactions and electric response are essential for accurate modeling in the context of material science, but capturing them efficiently remains a challenge in the relatively young field of atomistic machine learning. In this work, we introduced a machine-learning framework that jointly predicts static and dynamic (response) charge distributions, enabling accurate modelling of electric-field response and IR spectra.

Static Dielectric Permittivity Profiles and Coarse-Graining Approaches for Water in Graphene Slit Pores

Philipp Stärk, H. Stooß, P. Loche, D. J. Bonthuis, R. R. Netz, A. Schlaich

Chemical Physics Reviews 7 (1), 2026

DOI → Scholar

Exactly how the polarization of fluids inside of pores changes once a field is applied is surprisingly complex if one looks closely. This problem is not merely academic but very important for batteries, capacitors and in electrocatalysis. Not only does the response of the fluid depend on how close to a surface you are, it also depends on the direction one is looking. There is a well-formed framework to accurately understand what is happening, which are called dielectric profiles. However, due to the complexity of the theory, there was a need of a thorough review article that not only derives the formalism again, but also explains how the theory can be applied to predict and understand experimental results.

Phase Diagram and Criticality of the Modified Primitive Electrolyte Model in Bulk and in Inert and Conducting Confinement

Philipp Stärk, A. Schlaich

The Journal of Chemical Physics 164 (6), 2026

DOI → Scholar

Fluids can be in a peculiar state, where they are equally likely to be found in a dense, liquid phase and simultaneously in a dilute, gas phase (think droplets in a cloud). The only thing which determines if one phase or the other will be found is, more or less, chance. Interestingly, this behavior of fluid changes drastically if it is restricted to a small region, e.g. within a pore, such as in batteries. Understanding these systems is thus also very important if we want to build better batteries, capacitors or catalysts. Another exciting possibility would be to use this change in phases to store thermal energy in what is known as phase change materials. In this work I showcased my Wang-Landau algorithm implementation for a simple model system, called the "mRPM", which is a good model for an electrolyte as one would find in a capacitor or battery. We showed how the method allows for consistent predictions of the entire phase diagram of the fluid for a "free liquid" (bulk) and within confinement. One super interesting thing we found was that for very small pores, the liquid suddenly does not show this phase transition anymore.

The Three-Phase Contact Potential Difference Modulates the Water Surface Charge

V. Artemov, L. Frank, R. Doronin, P. Stärk, A. Schlaich, A. Andreev, et al.

The Journal of Physical Chemistry Letters 14 (20), 4796–4802, 2023

DOI → Scholar

The experimental group of Prof. Artemov measured something very interesting: tiny water droplets have quite different charges depending on pH and the type of material that the water is pushed out of. In particular they looked at Kapton ("likes water") and PTFE ("does not like water") channels and found remarkably different behavior between the two in the charges. In this study, I came up with a description of the polymeric surfaces and performed simulations of atomistic water with a particular focus on Oxonium and Hydroxide (the two most common charge carrying "forms of water"). Indeed we found that the behavior observed in experiments can be qualitatively predicted by these simulations, as the two polymer types attract and repel the two ions to quite different degrees.

Hydrodynamic Mobility Reversal of Squirmers Near Flat and Curved Surfaces

M. Kuron, Philipp Stärk, C. Holm, J. de Graaf

Soft Matter 15 (29), 5908–5920, 2019

DOI → Scholar

During my Bachelor thesis, I implemented a model that allows to describe the hydrodynamic interactions of bacteria with walls. This is an interesting topic to research as one would very much like to understand how we can keep bacteria where we want them (for example in bio-reactors) and keep them away from parts where we do not want them. During testing, we stumbled upon a very interesting and previously not observed pattern of swimming: In certain conditions, the bacteria circled a cylindrical obstacle in the backwards direction, even though it kept swimming forwards. We then performed extensive and large-scale parameter studies, finding out exactly which parameters resulted in this sort of peculiar behavior, which resulted in the bacterium getting stuck and for which parameters it kept on swimming forwards.

A Lattice Boltzmann Model for Squirmers

M. Kuron, Philipp Stärk, C. Burkard, J. de Graaf, C. Holm

The Journal of Chemical Physics 150 (14), 2019

DOI → Scholar

Because they are so small, for bacteria water "feels quite different" than it does for humans. Building on work of a previous master student, my Bachelor thesis was on the implementation of a bacterium-obstacle interactions model within the fluid dynamics solver framework called Lattice Boltzmann. The resulting versatile model of the hydrodynamics of swimming bacteria was summarized in this publication.
